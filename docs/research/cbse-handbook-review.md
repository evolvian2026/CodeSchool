## Summary

The books are **banks of maths puzzles, in multiple-choice form, arranged to follow the NCERT maths textbook chapter by chapter**, with one or two unplugged activities per class. From Class 6 there is also a four-chapter AI section.

- **No coding in any of the 12 books.**
- **No project materials in any of the 12 books**, although Classes 6-8 each give 40 hours to projects.
- **No assessment templates or rubrics in any book.**
- **AI:**
  - In Classes 6-8, the student books explain AI concepts in plain text, but the teacher books contain only the exercises and answer keys.
  - Only Class 8 has hands-on AI: three labs on Teachable Machine.

## Findings

### 1. The books are built around the maths lesson

Every book says it is "designed as a companion to the Mathematics textbook" and "follows the same chapter sequence". In Classes 3-5 the 50 hours a year are "integrated into existing Mathematics and 'The World Around Us' (TWAU) periods".

The chapter titles match the new NCERT maths textbooks one for one:
- **Class 3:** What's in a Name?, Toy Joy, Double Century, Vacation with My Nani Maa, Fun with Shapes, House of Hundreds I, Raksha Bandhan, Fair Share, House of Hundreds II, Fun at Class Party!, Filling and Lifting, Give and Take, Time Goes On, The Surajkund Fair.
- **Class 4:** Shapes Around Us, Hide and Seek, Patterns Around Us, Thousands Around Us, Sharing and Measuring, Measuring Length, The Cleanest Village, Weigh It Pour It, Equal Groups, Elephants Tigers and Leopards, Fun with Symmetry, Ticking Clocks and Turning Calendar, The Transport Museum, Data Handling.
- **Class 5:** We the Travellers I, Fractions, Angles as Turns, We the Travellers II, Far and Near, The Dairy Farm, Shapes and Patterns, Weight and Capacity, Coconut Farm, Symmetrical Designs, Grandmother's Quilt, Racing Seconds, Animal Jumps, Maps and Locations, Data Through Pictures.
- **Class 6:** Patterns in Mathematics, Lines and Angles, Number Play, Data Handling and Presentation, Prime Time, Perimeter and Area, Fractions, Playing with Constructions, Symmetry, The Other Side of Zero.
- **Class 7:** Large Numbers Around Us, Arithmetic Expressions, A Peek Beyond the Point, Expressions using Letter-Numbers, Parallel and Intersecting Lines, Number Play, A Tale of Three Intersecting Lines, Working with Fractions.
- **Class 8:** A Square and a Cube, Power Play, A Story of Numbers, Quadrilaterals, Number Play, We Distribute Yet Things Multiply, Proportional Reasoning.

**So what:** the unit a CBSE teacher plans by is the **NCERT maths chapter**, not a CT concept. Anything CodeSchool sells for Classes 3-8 should be **indexed by NCERT maths chapter** so the teacher can find "today's" material in one tap.

### 2. The puzzles are hard, even in Class 3

The questions are aptitude- and olympiad-style from the first book. Class 3 (age 8) includes:
- finding the heavier of 12 balls in the fewest weighings
- "which statements are needed to answer the question" (data sufficiency), three times
- multi-condition logic, such as a four-digit passcode from clues

Class 4 adds cube folding, queue ordering from clues and tracing button-press sequences. The student books give no hints and no answers, and there's no easier-to-harder progression within a chapter.

**So what:** this is where a **level-based structure adds real value**. The same chapter's puzzle skills can be broken into easier warm-ups, hints and worked examples, with harder levels for students who are ready. That supports the core hypothesis that schools will pay for scaffolding and practice, not for content.

### 3. Rule-following, branching and search start in Class 3, but always in puzzle form

Algorithm-style puzzles appear from the first book:
- **Class 3:**
  - "When on a WHITE block, he must skip the next TWO blocks. When on a BLACK block, he must skip the next ONE block" (*Filling and Lifting*): an if-then rule in puzzle form.
  - a frog that jumps 4 and slips back 2 (a loop)
  - a rabbit path that collects every fifth item
  - laying path tiles to connect Home to a destination
- **Class 4:** a rule with an exception (*Hide and Seek*), tracing a button sequence (*The Cleanest Village*), shortest paths.
- **Class 5:** the weighing-scale activity compares **linear search** with **divide-and-conquer**, and asks students why one is faster: real algorithm analysis.

Students only ever *follow* or *compare* algorithms. No book asks them to *write* instructions.

**So what:** CodeSchool's Band B levels can be presented as "**the puzzle in this chapter, now you build it**". Level B3 (Robo makes choices) fits naturally with the Class 3 skip rule, Class 4 *Hide and Seek* and *The Cleanest Village*. This also softens the earlier worry that B3 is "ahead" of CBSE: if-then rules are formally a Class 7 outcome, but they appear in puzzles from Class 3.

### 4. Nothing supports the coding competency, in any class

The CBSE curriculum lists "block-based coding (e.g. Scratch)" as a Class 3-5 competency, and goal CG-3 for Classes 3-5 is to "demonstrate understanding of basic concepts of computers and knowledge of hardware and software". **None of the Classes 3-5 books has content for either.**

The Class 8 book adds CG-5: "proficiency to use Computer & other devices, computer applications for learning". The Middle Stage books say they build "foundational knowledge on Coding, Data Analysis and Artificial Intelligence tools", but **none of the 12 books contains any coding**.

**But CBSE is telling schools that coding isn't needed.** The [28 April 2026 webinar](#what-the-cbse-webinar-adds-28-april-2026) presents the absence of coding as a design choice, not a gap:
- a Class 3 example is introduced with "No coding/software"
- the assessment slide says there is "no 'write a program'… no expectation of computer access"
- for Class 6: "Recognise — not build… They don't need to write code or train models"

**So what:** the coding gap is real on paper, since Scratch is still a listed Classes 3-5 competency, but schools may not feel it. Coding could be a weak buying reason by itself. It is more likely to sell as a **"now you build it" extension** of the puzzles, or through the parent channel, than as "CBSE requires it". This is a risk to test directly in the interviews (coordinator question 9, hypothesis H10).

### 5. Projects are 40% of Middle Stage time and have no materials in any book

In Classes 6, 7 and 8 alike, time is 40 hours of CT, 20 hours of AI, and 40 hours for "two required projects" of 20 hours each. The Class 6 book says students should "use AI tools and data analysis to create solutions for community or fictional city issues", and that teachers should use "clear rubrics". **Neither edition of any class contains a project brief, rubric or example.** Assessment includes practical examinations, project presentations and reflective journals.

The webinar gives one example topic per grade, all data projects ending in a presentation:
- Class 6: weather across states
- Class 7: "Nutri-Quest" weekly food planner
- Class 8: sports participation data

It also lists an **"AI Project list with rubrics" as "coming soon"**. Six months later (5 November 2026), the CT&AI page still doesn't link one.

**So what:** **ready-made, assessable project kits for Classes 6-8** cover the biggest unsupported block of time in the curriculum: 120 hours across three years. They are also where coding and AI can be used for real. This should be tested as a top buying reason in interviews (see hypothesis H10 in the interview plan). **The window may close:** once CBSE publishes its own project list and rubrics, a kit has to work with those topics rather than compete with them. Check the CT&AI page monthly.

### 6. AI: the student books teach it, the teacher books don't, and only Class 8 is hands-on

*This corrects the earlier version of this review, which had read only the teacher books for Classes 6-8.*

- **The student books contain the teaching.** Each AI chapter explains concepts in plain language, with Indian examples, then has exercises:
  - **Class 6:** AI versus automation, Alan Turing and the Turing Test, supervised/unsupervised/reinforcement learning, data types, patterns, and online safety (passwords, phishing, piracy, digital footprints).
  - **Class 7:** data science, computer vision and NLP; classification, regression and clustering explained with examples; six industry case studies; charts; bias.
  - **Class 8:** the AI project lifecycle, worked through with a "predict who scores above 75%" example including accuracy; AI in the environment, health, farming and education (for example ICMR's TB X-ray screening and the Pune waste app); fairness; responsible AI.
- **The teacher books contain only the exercises and answer keys.** There is no lesson plan, explanation or slide for the AI chapters, so the teacher has to teach from the student book. The webinar describes the teacher book as "everything in the student handbook, plus" solutions and prompts, which is not true of the Classes 6-8 AI sections. It also promises short **"Concept Handouts"** for the AI sections, "coming soon".
- **Classes 6 and 7 have no hands-on AI.** The Class 7 outcome "Apply basic predictive approaches/techniques to a small dataset" has no practical task behind it.
- **Class 8 has three step-by-step labs, all on Teachable Machine.** The book names Teachable Machine and Machine Learning for Kids as no-code platforms.
  1. **Waste classifier:** photograph 20-30 items each of plastic, paper and organic waste, train an image model, then test it with a webcam.
  2. **Voice commands:** record "Start", "Stop", "Help" and background noise 15-20 times each, train an audio model, then test it with other voices.
  3. **Cat/dog bias:** train on an unbalanced "Teachable Machines Cat-Dog Dataset in the Drive" (the text gives no link), record confidence scores, rebalance the data, retrain and compare.

  These labs need, per group, a computer with internet, a webcam and a microphone, and no offline alternative is offered.
- **First-edition roughness:** the Class 8 text describes a six-stage lifecycle, while its learning outcome and exercise use four stages ("Define Problem, Collect Data, Test AI Tools, Reflect and Improve").

**So what:** the AI gap is narrower than the earlier review said, but real:
- **Teacher support for the AI chapters**: scripts, slides and quick checks, since the teacher books have none.
- **Hands-on activities for Classes 6 and 7**, for example sorting a small dataset into groups (classification), predicting a value from past data (regression) and making the charts.
- **A device-light way to run the Class 8 labs** where there's one projector and no lab: teacher-led on a single screen, with the datasets supplied.

### 7. The activities are few, borrowed and depend on online links

Six years contain eight unplugged CT activities, one or two per class. They are well designed but mostly adapted from CS Unplugged. They rely on links to Google Docs worksheets, YouTube videos and tinyurl templates, and the Class 4 sorting network has to be chalked on the floor.

**So what:** a printable, offline **activity kit with a teacher script** for every NCERT chapter would fill the remaining chapters, which currently have only multiple-choice questions.

### 8. Assessment is continuous and nothing tracks it

Every book lists continuous, competency-based assessment, including written tests with CT puzzles, group activities and a **Teacher Observation Journal**. The Class 6 book adds "clear rubrics". No book gives a template, rubric or way to record it.

**So what:** a simple **observation journal and progress record**, mapped to the CBSE competencies and printable for report cards, is a small but concrete feature to test.

## What the CBSE webinar adds (28 April 2026)

Source: [webinar slides](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/CurriculumMain27/CTAI_WebinarPPT_2026-27.pdf), 45 slides, "Vision and Design of the CT-AI Curriculum for Classes 3–8". I read the slides in full but have not watched the [recording](https://youtube.com/live/KXYvvXZtYyk).

- **No coding, by design.** The slides repeat "no coding/software", "no 'write a program'" and "no expectation of computer access for assessment". The approach is "largely unplugged", and AI activities "use simple digital tools where available". See finding 4.
- **AI is about recognising, not building**, in Class 6 at least: "Recognise — not build… They don't need to write code or train models". Class 8's Teachable Machine labs go further than this.
- **Project examples, but no project list yet.** The slides give one data-project topic per grade and promise "AI Project list with rubrics", which is still not published. See finding 5.
- **More is "coming soon":**
  - a dedicated CT&AI page (now live)
  - "Concept Handouts" for the AI sections (not yet linked)
  - the project list with rubrics (not yet linked)
- **Who teaches:** in Classes 6-8, CT goes through Mathematics, Science **and Social Sciences** teachers ("similar to art-integrated learning"). AI literacy and the projects are "anchored by the Computer teacher".
- **Teacher training is CBSE's own CPD, not NISHTHA.** The 2026-27 annual training theme is "Computational Thinking and Understanding AI", with eight sub-themes. It is delivered through:
  - one-day district deliberations (6 CPD hours)
  - half-day lead-school sessions (3 hours)
  - online Board sessions
  - videos on the DD PM e-Vidya CBSE 15 channel
  - one-day regional workshops
- **Framing:** the slides cite Singapore, Finland, the US (AI4K12), the UAE, Australia and China as precedents. The hours are "indicative — schools can adjust".

**So what:**
- CBSE has set expectations low on technology, which suits low-device schools. It also means a CodeSchool pitch built on "CBSE requires coding" would contradict the Board's own message.
- The strongest openings are the ones CBSE itself admits are unfinished: **project lists with rubrics and AI concept support**. Both are promised but not yet delivered.
- Teacher training is short (one-day workshops). That supports the case for **scripted, in-product teacher guidance**.

## What this means for CodeSchool

**Don't compete with the books. Sit on top of them.**

| CBSE gives (free) | CodeSchool could give (paid) |
|---|---|
| Puzzle banks by maths chapter, Classes 3-8 | The same chapter skills as **levels**, with hints, easier on-ramps and auto-checked practice, on phone, projector or paper |
| No coding in any class | **Block-coding levels** tied to each chapter's puzzles ("now you build it"), covering the Scratch competency |
| 8 unplugged activities across six classes | An activity for every chapter, with printable kits and a teacher script |
| No project materials in any class | **Project kits for Classes 6-8** (40 hours a year): briefs, rubrics, examples and assessment |
| AI teaching text in the student books; answer keys only for teachers | **Teacher scripts and slides** for every AI chapter |
| Hands-on AI only in Class 8, and only with a device, camera and internet | **Hands-on AI for Classes 6-7**, and a device-light, projector-friendly version of the Class 8 labs |
| A "Teacher Observation Journal" with no template | A **progress journal and dashboard** mapped to the CBSE competencies |

This sharpens the positioning in the research report: CodeSchool is **the easiest way to deliver the parts of CBSE CT&AI that CBSE didn't provide**, namely coding, projects, AI teacher support, hands-on AI on low-end equipment, and assessment. It works alongside the free books instead of replacing them.
