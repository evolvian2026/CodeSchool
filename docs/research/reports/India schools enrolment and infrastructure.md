# CBSE's mandate meets India's device gap

India's school system is huge, mostly government-run and still mostly offline. **UDISE+ 2025-26 counts 14,66,682 schools.** Of these, 10,05,245 (68.5%) are government, 79,261 (5.4%) government-aided, 3,41,689 (23.3%) private unaided and 40,487 (2.8%) "others". Together they serve **24.72 crore students and employ 1.03 crore teachers** ([EFA key indicators](https://educationforallinindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/UDISEPlus-all-india-national-key-indicators-2020-21-to-2025-26-aruncmehta.pdf)). Government schools hold under half of Classes 1-12 enrolment (49.7%), and private unaided schools hold 38.0% (40.0% including pre-primary). The Classes 3-8 band that CodeSchool targets has an **estimated 12.4 crore students**. Only **33,248 schools, about 2.3% of the total, are CBSE-affiliated** ([CBSE SARAS](https://saras.cbse.gov.in/saras/AffiliatedList/ListOfSchdirReport), accessed 6 Nov 2026). Those schools carry the Computational Thinking and AI (CT&AI) requirement for Classes 3-8 from 2026-27, so they are CodeSchool's core market. They hold an estimated 45 lakh to 1.4 crore Classes 3-8 students, with a central estimate of about 88 lakh, or about Rs 460 crore a year at the Rs 449/599 pack prices. A far larger voluntary pool sits in state-board private schools: about 4 crore students. That pool is budget-constrained and short of devices. School digital coverage is growing quickly, but most of it is presence, not capacity. 67.4% of schools report internet and 63.1% a computer usable for teaching. Only 36.5% have a working desktop, 22.1% a working projector and 33.9% a smart classroom. Government schools trail far behind, with 25.5% holding a working desktop ([EFA digital divide](https://educationforallinindia.com/the-digital-divide-in-indian-schools-what-udise-2025-26-reveals-about-ict-infrastructure/)). The Offline Programme is therefore CodeSchool's main product, not a fallback. The Online Programme fits roughly the 40-53% of private and aided schools with a usable device and connection (an estimate). Every trend in this report has to be read against UDISE+'s 2022-23 methodology break, and every device figure against the fact that UDISE+ records whether a school has a device, not how many.

*Units and conventions: 1 lakh = 100,000; 1 crore = 10 million. "2025-26" means the UDISE+ round with a 31 March 2026 data cut-off, released on 7 July 2026 ([Vision IAS](https://visionias.in/current-affairs/news-today/2026-07-08/society/union-ministry-of-education-released-the-unified-district-information-system-for-education-plus-udise-2025-26-report)). The official UDISE+ booklets were too large to fetch, so most national figures come from Prof. Arun C. Mehta's compilation of the official data (former NIEPA; cited as "EFA"), which this research checked for internal consistency. Every figure marked **(est.)** is this report's own calculation, not an official statistic.*

## Government runs 69% of schools but teaches under half the pupils

The school count has fallen by about 42,000 since 2020-21. Nearly all of the fall is government-school consolidation. The private unaided count dipped after 2021-22 and has since recovered almost to its 2020-21 level. Between 2024-25 and 2025-26, government schools fell by 8,077 (-0.8%) while private unaided schools rose by 2,106 (+0.6%) ([EFA 2025-26 analysis](https://educationforallinindia.com/udiseplus-2025-26-analysis-by-aruncmehta-educationforallinindia/)). Zero-enrolment schools fell from 7,993 to 5,663 in a year, and West Bengal accounts for 4,133 of the remainder ([EFA single-teacher schools](https://educationforallinindia.com/single-teacher-schools-schools-without-enrolment-udise-2025-26/)).

### Enrolment is drifting down, and the 2022-23 fall is mostly a data artefact

Classes 1-12 enrolment was **23.23 crore in 2025-26**. Adding pre-primary gives 24.72 crore. Primary enrolment (Classes 1-5) is shrinking by about 2-2.5% a year, while upper primary (Classes 6-8) is roughly flat ([EFA 2025-26 analysis](https://educationforallinindia.com/udiseplus-2025-26-analysis-by-aruncmehta-educationforallinindia/)). The sharp 1.41 crore drop between 2021-22 and 2022-23 coincides with UDISE+ switching to **student-wise, Aadhaar-linked records**. The Ministry says this switch "weeded out ghost students" enrolled in both a government and a private school ([Outlook](https://www.outlookindia.com/national/enrolment-in-schools-declined-by-over-a-crore-shows-udise-report)). Demographics account for the slower decline since then: the projected 6-10 population fell from 11.49 crore to 11.40 crore in one year ([EFA 2025-26 analysis](https://educationforallinindia.com/udiseplus-2025-26-analysis-by-aruncmehta-educationforallinindia/)).

On a Classes 1-12 basis, management shares in 2025-26 were government 49.66% (11.54 crore), aided 10.45% (2.43 crore), private unaided 37.97% (8.82 crore) and others 1.91% (0.44 crore) ([EFA NEP distribution](https://educationforallinindia.com/analysing-distribution-of-udise-2025-26-schools-enrolment-and-teachers-towards-nep-2020-goals/)). The widely quoted headline that **"private schools cross 40%"** includes pre-primary, which is about 71% private ([EFA private 40%](https://educationforallinindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/private-schools-cross-40-percent-enrolment-mark-udise-2025-26.html)). For a Classes 3-8 product, the 38% figure is the right one to use.

Household surveys put the government share higher than the UDISE+ school census does. The NSS Comprehensive Modular Survey on Education (Apr-Jun 2025) found **55.9% of enrolment in government schools** and 31.9% in private unaided recognised schools. ASER 2024 found 66.8% of rural 6-14-year-olds in government schools, down from 72.9% in 2022 ([ASER 2024 via PARI](https://ruralindiaonline.org/en/library/resource/annual-status-of-education-report-rural-2024)). A census of school records and a survey of households measure different things, so neither is wrong. Both say the same thing for a coding programme: in rural India, private schools serve about one child in three.

### Most schools are small, and the Classes 3-8 band crosses school types

UDISE+ classifies a school by the highest level it offers. In 2025-26 there were 7,22,498 primary, 4,32,374 upper-primary, 1,43,200 secondary and 1,68,610 higher-secondary schools ([EFA NEP distribution](https://educationforallinindia.com/analysing-distribution-of-udise-2025-26-schools-enrolment-and-teachers-towards-nep-2020-goals/)). Primary-category schools are numerous but thin. In 2024-25 they were **49.6% of schools and held 48.0% of enrolment, but only 23.4% of teachers** ([UDISE+ 2024-25 Table 2.1](https://educationforallinindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/State-wise-share-of-schools-enrolment-and-teachers-UDISEPlus-2024-25-Ministry-of-Education.pdf)). **36.4% of all schools had 50 or fewer students** in 2024-25 ([EFA small schools](https://educationforallinindia.com/challenges-of-small-schools-in-india-analysis-of-udise-2024-25-enrolment-data/)). Every primary school teaches Classes 3-5 and every upper-primary school teaches Classes 6-8. This report therefore estimates that 12-14 lakh schools teach part of the 3-8 band. Only composite schools (Classes 1-8, 1-10 or 1-12) teach all of it, probably 4.5-7 lakh schools **(est.)**; UDISE+ Table 3.1, which would settle this, was not readable.

### Private schooling is mostly budget schooling

The NSS education survey (2025) puts average household spending at **Rs 25,002 a year per non-government student** (rural Rs 19,554, urban Rs 31,782), against Rs 2,863 per government student ([Dataful](https://insights.dataful.in/articles/private-schooling-costs-nearly-9-times-more-than-government-education-nss-survey)). The latest fee distribution is older, from Central Square Foundation's analysis of 2017-18 NSS data: **70% of private-school students paid under Rs 1,000 a month and 45% under Rs 500** ([CSF fact sheet](https://centralsquarefoundation.org/Fact-sheet-State-of-the-Sector-Report-on-Private-Schools-in-India.pdf)). On those figures, a Rs 449 pack costs about 2.3% of a rural private family's annual schooling spend and 1.4% of an urban family's. For a school charging Rs 500 a month, it equals 7-8% of a year's tuition **(est.)**. The pack is affordable as a booklist item, but it will be noticed.

## Six private-heavy states hold half the private Classes 3-8 pool

Enrolment is concentrated. The 15 largest states hold about 87% of students, and Uttar Pradesh alone holds 17.3% ([UDISE+ 2024-25 Table 2.1](https://educationforallinindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/State-wise-share-of-schools-enrolment-and-teachers-UDISEPlus-2024-25-Ministry-of-Education.pdf)). Management mix varies far more than size. Telangana, Haryana, Andhra Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab and Rajasthan have **more than half of enrolment in private unaided schools**. West Bengal (6.3%) and Bihar (13.5-14.8%) are overwhelmingly government. Maharashtra and Kerala are aided-school systems ([EFA private 40%](https://educationforallinindia.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/private-schools-cross-40-percent-enrolment-mark-udise-2025-26.html)).

[Circular Acad-15/2026](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Circulars/2026/15_Circular_2026.pdf) (1 April 2026) says schools are "advised" to adopt it, at **50 hours a year in Classes 3-5** (inside Mathematics and TWAU) and **100 hours in Classes 6-8** (40 CT, 20 AI, 40 projects). [Circular Acad-18/2026](https://cbseacademic.nic.in/web_material/Circulars/2026/18_Circular_2026.pdf) uses stronger wording (the curriculum "mandates" its content) and posts **free student and teacher resource books** that schools "should download". All twelve books were reviewed for this project ([CBSE handbook review](../cbse-handbook-review.md)): they need no devices, but they include no coding, project materials or rubrics. **CodeSchool therefore competes against a zero-price official baseline** and must sell on what the free books lack (coding, project kits, AI activities, teacher scripts and assessment), not on content alone. The Budget's AI-related items all target older students or higher education: a Rs 500 crore AI Centre of Excellence for education, the tinkering labs, BharatNet for secondary schools, and AVGC content-creator labs in 15,000 secondary schools announced in Budget 2026-27 ([MoE Budget release](https://education.gov.in/sites/upload_files/mhrd/files/PIB2098805.pdf); [ThePrint](https://theprint.in/economy/budgets-orange-economy-push-why-govt-is-backing-animation-gaming-vfx-comics/2842625/)).

## Conclusion

The CBSE mandate creates CodeSchool's first market, and it is narrow and dense: about 33,000 schools, roughly 2% of India's total, holding on the order of 88 lakh Classes 3-8 pupils (est.). Its size is uncertain within a factor of three, and the cheapest way to narrow that range is the school-level data UDISE+ already holds: board, class range and rural/urban location for every school. The larger prize is voluntary. Segment D, about 4 crore state-board private pupils, outweighs the entire CBSE segment several times over, and about half of all private Classes 3-8 pupils sit in six states, a quarter in Uttar Pradesh alone (est.). But this segment is price-sensitive and device-poor. That inverts the usual edtech logic. The printed workbook and unplugged kit are the scalable core, and the Online Programme is a premium tier for roughly the 10,000-14,000 CBSE schools and the aided and private schools that have a working screen.

The infrastructure data also implies a design rule. School connectivity is spreading faster than devices, and Bihar's 91% internet against 38% teaching computers is the clearest case. New central money is going to secondary schools. The device CodeSchool can most often count on in a Classes 3-8 classroom over the next two years is therefore a teacher's phone or a single shared screen, not a lab. A programme built for "one device at the front, paper on every desk" serves the whole system. One built for lab rotations serves perhaps a fifth of government schools and half of private ones. The free CBSE resource books and the official DIKSHA, TV and radio channels are not rivals to price against. They are a zero-cost baseline that sets what schools expect, and CodeSchool's value has to come from what the government does not supply: scripted teacher support, kits and assessment.
